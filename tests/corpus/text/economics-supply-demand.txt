3. Supply, Demand, and Market Equilibrium

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers of a good together. Economists explain the prices that emerge in competitive markets with two relationships. The law of demand states that, other things being equal, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises. The law of supply states that the quantity supplied rises as the price rises, because a higher price makes it profitable for firms to produce more.

It is important to separate a movement along a curve from a shift of the curve. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. A change in anything else that affects buyers shifts the whole curve. These other factors include consumer income, tastes, expectations, and the prices of related goods. For a normal good, demand increases when income rises; for an inferior good, it decreases. Two goods are substitutes if a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other, as with tea and coffee. They are complements if a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other, as with printers and ink.

The equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At any higher price there is a surplus, and sellers who cannot find buyers cut their prices. At any lower price there is a shortage, and buyers bid the price up. In this way the market tends to return to equilibrium without anyone directing it. Adam Smith described this tendency as the invisible hand.

How strongly buyers respond to a price change is measured by the price elasticity of demand, which is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic when this value is greater than one in absolute terms and inelastic when it is less than one. Goods with close substitutes tend to have elastic demand, while necessities such as insulin tend to have inelastic demand. This matters for sellers: when demand is inelastic, raising the price increases total revenue, but when it is elastic the same increase reduces it.

Governments sometimes prevent prices from adjusting. A price ceiling is a legal maximum price; if it is set below the equilibrium it creates a persistent shortage, as rent control has done in many cities. A price floor is a legal minimum price, and one set above the equilibrium, such as a minimum wage above the market wage, creates a surplus. Such policies may help the people they are aimed at, but they also produce a deadweight loss, which is the value of the trades that no longer take place.

The benefit that buyers receive from a market is called consumer surplus. It is the difference between what they would have been willing to pay and what they actually pay. Producer surplus is the difference between the price sellers receive and their cost of production. Together these make up total surplus, and in a competitive market without externalities the equilibrium quantity maximizes it.
