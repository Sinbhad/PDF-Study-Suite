Chapter 6: How Memory Works

Psychologists usually describe memory as three linked activities. Encoding is the process of converting what we perceive into a form the brain can store. Storage refers to keeping that information over time, and retrieval is the act of bringing it back when it is needed. A failure at any one of these points looks, from the outside, like forgetting.

In 1968 Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin proposed that information passes through three separate stores. Sensory memory holds a detailed copy of what the senses have just taken in, but only for a second or two. Whatever we pay attention to moves on to short-term memory, which is limited in both duration and capacity. George Miller famously estimated that it holds about seven items, plus or minus two. People get around this limit by chunking, which means grouping separate items into larger meaningful units. Unless it is rehearsed, material in short-term memory fades within about twenty seconds.

Later researchers argued that this account was too passive. Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch replaced the idea of a single short-term store with working memory, a system that both holds and manipulates information. In their model the central executive directs attention, while the phonological loop deals with spoken and written words and the visuospatial sketchpad deals with images and locations. They later added the episodic buffer, which ties these together with long-term knowledge.

Long-term memory appears to have no fixed limit. It is commonly divided into explicit memory, which we can consciously state, and implicit memory, which shows itself in behavior without conscious recall. Explicit memory includes episodic memory for personal events and semantic memory for general facts. Procedural memory, such as knowing how to ride a bicycle, is the best-known kind of implicit memory. Damage to the hippocampus often prevents patients from forming new explicit memories, yet they can still learn new motor skills. This suggests that the two systems rely on different parts of the brain.

How well something is remembered depends heavily on how it was encoded. According to the levels of processing theory, thinking about the meaning of a word produces a stronger trace than noticing its sound or shape. The encoding specificity principle states that recall is best when the conditions at retrieval match those present during learning. This is why returning to the room where you studied can make an answer suddenly come back.

Forgetting has several causes. Hermann Ebbinghaus showed that most loss happens soon after learning and then levels off, a pattern known as the forgetting curve. Proactive interference occurs when older memories disrupt the recall of newer ones, whereas retroactive interference occurs when new learning disrupts the recall of older material. Memories can also be altered after the fact. In studies by Elizabeth Loftus, people who were asked misleading questions about a film of a car accident later remembered details that had never been shown. She called this the misinformation effect, and it is one reason courts treat eyewitness testimony with caution.
