The French Revolution, 1789-1799

By the late 1780s the French monarchy was close to bankruptcy. Decades of war, including support for the American colonies, had left the crown deeply in debt, and a poor harvest in 1788 pushed the price of bread beyond the reach of many families. In an attempt to raise new taxes, Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General, an assembly of the clergy, the nobility and the commoners that had not met since 1614. It opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789.

The delegates of the Third Estate, who represented the commoners, objected that each estate was to have a single vote. On 17 June they declared themselves the National Assembly. Three days later, finding their meeting hall locked, they gathered in a nearby indoor tennis court and swore not to separate until France had a constitution. This is known as the Tennis Court Oath. Rumors that the king intended to dissolve the Assembly by force spread through Paris, and on 14 July a crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison. Its fall became the symbol of the Revolution.

That summer the Assembly dismantled much of the old order. On 4 August it abolished feudal privileges, and on 26 August it adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed that men are born free and equal in rights. In October thousands of Parisian women marched to Versailles and forced the royal family to move to Paris. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy, passed in 1790, placed the Catholic Church under state control and divided the country.

In June 1791 the king and his family tried to flee and were arrested at Varennes. The attempt destroyed what trust remained in him. France went to war with Austria and Prussia in April 1792, and after early defeats a Parisian crowd attacked the Tuileries Palace on 10 August. The monarchy was suspended. A new assembly, the National Convention, proclaimed the First Republic on 22 September 1792. Louis XVI was tried for treason and guillotined on 21 January 1793.

Power now passed to the most radical faction, the Jacobins. Led by Maximilien Robespierre, they dominated the Committee of Public Safety, which had been created to defend the Republic against its enemies at home and abroad. During the period known as the Reign of Terror, from September 1793 to July 1794, revolutionary courts sent roughly 17,000 people to the guillotine. Their rivals, the more moderate Girondins, were among the first victims. The Terror ended only when the Convention turned on Robespierre himself; he was executed on 28 July 1794 in what is called the Thermidorian Reaction.

A five-man executive known as the Directory governed from 1795. It was corrupt and depended on the army to survive. On 9 November 1799 a young general, Napoleon Bonaparte, overthrew it in the Coup of 18 Brumaire and made himself First Consul. Historians usually treat this event as the end of the Revolution.
