7.2 Cellular Respiration

Cells cannot use the energy stored in glucose directly. Instead, they transfer it in small steps to adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the molecule that powers almost every energy-requiring reaction in the cell. This process is called cellular respiration, and in eukaryotes most of it takes place inside the mitochondria. It can be divided into three stages, each of which depends on the products of the one before it.

The first stage, glycolysis, occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. During glycolysis one six-carbon glucose molecule is split into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. This yields a net gain of two ATP and two molecules of NADH. Because it happens in nearly all living organisms, biologists think it evolved very early, before the atmosphere contained much oxygen.

If oxygen is available, pyruvate is transported into the mitochondrial matrix. There it is converted into acetyl-CoA, and one carbon is released as carbon dioxide. Acetyl-CoA then enters the citric acid cycle, which is also known as the Krebs cycle. In this cycle the remaining carbons are oxidized completely, and their electrons are passed to the carriers NAD+ and FAD. These carriers become NADH and FADH2. Although the cycle itself makes only two ATP per glucose, it is essential because they deliver high-energy electrons to the final stage.

That final stage is oxidative phosphorylation. The electron transport chain is a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons move along the chain, the complexes pump protons out of the matrix and into the intermembrane space. This creates a steep concentration gradient across the membrane. The protons can flow back only through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and as they do so it attaches a phosphate group to ADP. Peter Mitchell named this mechanism chemiosmosis. Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor; it combines with electrons and protons to form water. Without it the chain backs up and stops.

When oxygen is absent, many cells switch to fermentation. Fermentation is an anaerobic pathway that regenerates NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue. In muscle cells this produces lactate, whereas in yeast it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. Such pathways release far less energy than aerobic respiration does: only two ATP per glucose instead of roughly thirty. For this reason, organisms that rely on them must consume glucose much faster.

The rate of respiration is controlled by feedback inhibition. When ATP levels are high, ATP binds to the enzyme phosphofructokinase and slows glycolysis down. When they fall, the inhibition is released and the pathway speeds up again. In this way the cell matches its production of ATP to its demand for it.
